From Skeuomorphism to Flat Design—and Back Toward Tactility

Trace the movement from realistic materials and physical metaphors to flat interfaces, then toward the restrained depth of Flat 2.0, neumorphism, glassmorphism, and claymorphism.

By Web Design Trend Catalog23 July 20265 sections

When the interface looked like an object

Aqua interfaces, Web 2.0 gloss, and skeuomorphism made digital surfaces appear polished, dimensional, and touchable. Buttons used highlights, shadows, and beveled edges. Notebooks displayed page edges and bindings. Media collections sat on simulated shelves. Controls resembled switches, knobs, and dials.

These treatments did more than decorate a screen. Physical metaphors helped explain unfamiliar digital actions by connecting them to objects people already understood.

Flat design removes the simulation

Flat design stripped away much of the texture, reflection, and simulated depth associated with rich interfaces. Solid color fields, simple geometric icons, sans-serif type, rectangular controls, and generous negative space became dominant visual tools.

The reduction produced clear, scalable systems and aligned well with responsive interfaces. It also created a usability problem when clickable controls became visually indistinguishable from static content.

Flat 2.0 restores useful depth

Flat 2.0 kept the economy of flat design while reintroducing selected shadows, gradients, button backgrounds, layered cards, and explicit states. Depth was no longer used to imitate every physical surface; it was used selectively to clarify hierarchy and interaction.

Many interfaces commonly described as “flat” today are closer to this moderated form.

The return of tactility takes several forms

Neumorphism makes controls appear softly raised from or pressed into a nearly identical background. Glassmorphism uses translucent panels, blur, thin borders, and layered color. Claymorphism builds playful depth with inflated forms, matte surfaces, pastel colors, and toy-like objects.

These styles do not simply restore skeuomorphism. They reinterpret tactility through the visual language and production capabilities of contemporary interfaces.

A useful comparison question

When comparing these movements, look beyond whether a design “has shadows.” Ask what the depth is doing. Is it explaining a physical metaphor, signaling an interactive state, organizing conceptual planes, creating atmosphere, or expressing a playful brand personality?

That functional distinction is often more informative than the surface effect alone.
